History & Machines

Before qubits and Bloch spheres, a quick tour of why computing needed a new idea at all — and a first look at what a classical machine and a quantum machine actually look like, side by side.

A Brief History of Computing

The first "computers" weren't electronic at all. Mechanical calculators — gears, levers, and cranks — could add and multiply, but every operation was a physical, moving part clanking through its motion. They were reliable in principle, but slow, bulky, and limited to arithmetic.

Vacuum tubes changed that in the mid-20th century. Machines like ENIAC used thousands of glass tubes as switches, finally giving computing the speed of electricity instead of the speed of gears. The catch: tubes were power-hungry, generated enormous heat, and burned out constantly — a single failed tube among thousands could halt the whole machine.

The transistor, invented in 1947, replaced fragile glowing glass with a small, solid-state switch. Transistors were smaller, cooler, cheaper, and dramatically more reliable. It was the transistor that made computing something you could put on a desk instead of in a warehouse.

Then came the integrated circuit — etching many transistors onto a single chip — and with it, Moore's Law: roughly every two years, engineers doubled the number of transistors that could fit on a chip of the same size. That trend held for decades and is the entire reason your phone is more powerful than a room-sized 1970s mainframe.

But that doubling is hitting a wall. Modern transistors are now just a few nanometers across — a handful of atoms wide. At that scale, quantum effects like electron tunneling start to leak current where it shouldn't be, heat becomes nearly impossible to dissipate, and the classical assumption that a bit is cleanly "0 or 1" starts to strain against physics itself.

That wall is exactly why quantum computing matters. Instead of fighting quantum effects at the atomic scale, quantum computers embrace them as the basic resource — a fundamentally different way to compute, not just a smaller, faster version of the same one.

Bit vs Qubit — Now in 3D on Both Sides

A classical bit is a switch: off or on, 0 or 1, full stop. A qubit is a point that can sit anywhere on the surface of a sphere — not just at the two poles. Below, both are rendered in 3D so you can compare them on equal footing.

The core contrast: a classical bit can only ever be at one of two discrete states — flip it and it snaps instantly from one face to the other. A qubit's state is a continuous point on a whole sphere of possibilities, and that continuum is where quantum algorithms find their extra power.

What Is a Quantum Computer & How Does It Work?

At the most basic level, a quantum computer is built from qubits — the quantum equivalent of a bit, but capable of existing in a blend of 0 and 1 at once. Two properties make qubits genuinely powerful:

Superposition

A qubit can be in a mixture of |0⟩ and |1⟩ simultaneously, with certain probabilities of measuring each outcome. This lets a set of qubits represent many possible combinations of 0s and 1s at once, instead of just one.

Entanglement

Two or more qubits can become correlated in a way that has no classical equivalent — measuring one instantly tells you something about the others, no matter how they're arranged. This is the resource that lets quantum algorithms coordinate information in ways classical bits simply can't.

Put simply: a quantum processor is a machine that manipulates probability amplitudes via gates — precise, reversible operations — until a final measurement collapses that rich, probabilistic state down to an ordinary, classical answer: a string of 0s and 1s.

An honest caveat: quantum computers are not "faster classical computers." They won't speed up everyday tasks like browsing the web or running a spreadsheet. Their advantage is narrow but real — specific problem classes like search, simulation, optimization, and factoring, which is exactly where Part 4: Real Algorithms (Chapter 7) picks up later in this book.
