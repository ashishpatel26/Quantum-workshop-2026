Chapter 2 · Talk

Why Quantum?

Classical computers are wonderfully powerful — and fundamentally limited by how bits work. Quantum computers don't just go faster; they compute differently.

The Spinning Coin: Superposition

A classical bit is like a coin lying flat on a table: it's either heads or tails, full stop, and you can look at it any time without changing that fact.

A qubit in superposition is like a coin spinning in the air. While it's spinning, it isn't "really" heads or tails — it's in a blend of both possibilities, with certain odds of landing each way. The moment you slap your hand down and look (measure it), the spinning stops and it collapses to one definite outcome: heads or tails. Before that measurement, though, the spinning coin was doing something a flat coin never could — existing as a mixture of both states, and quantum algorithms exploit that mixture before it collapses.

Linked Coins: Entanglement

Now imagine two coins that are magically linked so that however one lands, the other is guaranteed to land the opposite way — instantly, even if you take them to opposite sides of the room. Flip one, and you instantly know the other's outcome, without touching it. That's the everyday-language version of entanglement: two qubits sharing a single quantum state, so that measuring one immediately tells you about the other. We'll build this for real with Qiskit in Chapters 5 and 6.

Classical CPU vs Quantum Processor

Here's the comparison the whole workshop hangs on: a classical bit is a switch that's either off or on, full stop. A qubit is a point that can sit anywhere on the surface of a sphere — not just at the two poles.

Why Should You Care? Real Applications

Drug Discovery

Simulating molecules is exponentially hard classically because you're tracking quantum interactions between electrons. A quantum computer can represent those interactions natively, potentially speeding up the search for new medicines.

Optimization

Logistics, finance portfolios, and scheduling problems often boil down to searching huge solution spaces. Quantum algorithms like Grover's (Chapter 7) offer provable speedups for exactly this kind of unstructured search.

Cryptography

Shor's algorithm (also Chapter 7) can factor large numbers efficiently — the exact math problem that makes RSA encryption secure today. That's why "quantum-safe" cryptography is already a hot research area.
