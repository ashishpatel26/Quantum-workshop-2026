Chapter 4 · Talk

Entanglement

Two qubits can become linked so tightly that measuring one instantly tells you about the other — no matter how far apart they are. This is the single most "quantum" phenomenon in the whole workshop.

Bell Pairs

The simplest entangled state is called a Bell pair: two qubits prepared so that they will always be measured as the same value — both 0, or both 1 — but which value is only decided at the moment of measurement. Before that, neither qubit has a definite state on its own; only the pair as a whole is well-defined.

Classical Correlation vs Quantum Entanglement

You might think "so what, correlated things exist classically too." Consider two coins glued together back-to-back before being separated and mailed to two cities: if you know one is heads-up, you instantly know the other is tails-up. That's classical correlation — but the outcome was decided the moment you glued them, it just wasn't revealed until later. There's a "hidden variable" (which way they were glued) that determined everything in advance.

Entangled qubits are different: physics experiments (testing what's called Bell's inequality) show there is no hidden variable decided in advance. The outcome genuinely doesn't exist until measurement — and yet the two qubits' outcomes are still perfectly correlated. That combination (genuinely undetermined, yet perfectly correlated) has no classical explanation. It's not that we don't know the pre-existing answer; there simply isn't one until you look.

Classical Correlation (glued coins)Quantum Entanglement (Bell pair)
Outcome fixed in advance, just hiddenOutcome undetermined until measurement
Explained by a hidden variableProvably has no hidden variable (Bell's inequality)
Correlation is "boring," classical statisticsCorrelation is stronger than any classical model allows
Not faster-than-light communication: even though measuring one qubit instantly affects our knowledge of the other, you can't use entanglement to send a message faster than light — the outcome you see is still random, and there's no way to control what the other party sees. It's a subtle point worth remembering if you read about entanglement elsewhere.
