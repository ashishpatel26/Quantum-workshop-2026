Noise & Real Hardware

Every circuit you have run so far was perfect — because the simulator is perfect. Real quantum hardware is not. This chapter is the reality check.

The Lie of Omission

When you ran the Bell state in Chapter 6 and saw a clean split between '00' and '11' with nothing else, that was true — of an idealised mathematical model. An actual quantum processor is a physical object: superconducting circuits chilled to a few thousandths of a degree above absolute zero, or trapped ions held by lasers. Physical objects leak, drift, and get nudged by their surroundings.

The simulator is not lying to you, but it is quietly leaving something out. On real hardware, that same Bell circuit produces a few percent of '01' and '10' — outcomes that are supposed to be impossible.

The Four Ways Things Go Wrong

Relaxation (T1)

A qubit in |1⟩ holds more energy than one in |0⟩, and nature prefers low energy. Left alone, the qubit gradually leaks that energy into its surroundings and decays toward |0⟩. The characteristic time this takes is called T1. If your circuit takes longer than T1, your |1⟩ quietly becomes a |0⟩ before you ever measure it.

Dephasing (T2)

The qubit keeps its 0-versus-1 balance but loses track of its phase — the very quantity Chapter 7 showed was doing all the real work. Stray fields and tiny frequency wobbles scramble the timing. The timescale is called T2. Once phase is gone, interference no longer works, and the qubit degrades into something that behaves like an ordinary random bit.

Gate Error

A gate is applied by firing a precisely shaped microwave or laser pulse. Shape it 1% wrong and you get a rotation of 179° instead of 180°. Each gate is almost the one you asked for, and the small discrepancies accumulate across the circuit. Two-qubit gates like CNOT are typically an order of magnitude noisier than single-qubit gates.

Readout Error

Even a qubit that survived the whole circuit perfectly can be misread. Measurement distinguishes two very faint signals, and sometimes a genuine 0 is reported as a 1 or vice versa. This one is purely a reporting error — the state was fine; the classical bit you got back was not.

Simulating Noise Yourself

You do not need access to a real device to see this. Qiskit Aer lets you attach a noise model to the simulator, so you can run the identical circuit ideally and noisily and compare the two histograms directly.

from qiskit import QuantumCircuit, transpile
from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator
from qiskit_aer.noise import NoiseModel, depolarizing_error
from qiskit.visualization import plot_histogram

# The same Bell circuit from Chapter 6
qc = QuantumCircuit(2, 2)
qc.h(0)
qc.cx(0, 1)
qc.measure([0, 1], [0, 1])

# --- 1. Ideal run ---
ideal_sim = AerSimulator()
ideal_counts = ideal_sim.run(transpile(qc, ideal_sim), shots=2000).result().get_counts()

# --- 2. Build a noise model ---
noise_model = NoiseModel()
# 2% depolarizing error on every 1-qubit 'h' gate
noise_model.add_all_qubit_quantum_error(depolarizing_error(0.02, 1), ["h"])
# 5% depolarizing error on every 2-qubit 'cx' gate (2-qubit gates are noisier)
noise_model.add_all_qubit_quantum_error(depolarizing_error(0.05, 2), ["cx"])

# --- 3. Noisy run ---
noisy_sim = AerSimulator(noise_model=noise_model)
noisy_counts = noisy_sim.run(transpile(qc, noisy_sim), shots=2000).result().get_counts()

print("ideal:", ideal_counts)   # {'00': ~1000, '11': ~1000}
print("noisy:", noisy_counts)   # {'00': ~960, '11': ~955, '01': ~45, '10': ~40}

plot_histogram([ideal_counts, noisy_counts], legend=["ideal", "noisy"])
What is "depolarizing" error? It is the simplest useful stand-in for "something went wrong here." With some probability, the gate discards the qubit's actual state and replaces it with a completely random one. It is not a faithful model of any specific physical mechanism, but it captures the essential effect — a bit of the quantum information is destroyed at every gate — and it is the standard first noise model to reach for.

Small but real '01' and '10' bars — the correlation has been chipped away.

Those two little bars are the whole story of NISQ-era quantum computing. They are the visible proof that noise erodes exactly the correlation the algorithm depends on. Deepen the circuit — more gates, more CNOTs — and the errors compound until the signal is buried and the histogram approaches uniform noise.

Simulator versus Real QPU

Aer SimulatorReal Quantum Processor
Execution substrateClassical CPU doing linear algebra on your laptopPhysical qubits: superconducting circuits near absolute zero, or trapped ions
NoiseNone by default; you can opt in to a modelAlways present, drifts day to day, must be recalibrated
Inspect the statevector?Yes — every amplitude and phase is readable at any pointNo. Measurement collapses the state; you only ever get counts
ScalingMemory doubles with every qubit — roughly 30 qubits is a laptop's practical ceilingScales naturally with qubit count, but coherence and error rates become the real limit
RepeatabilityDeterministic given a seed; identical results every runVaries between runs and between calibration cycles
Cost & accessFree, instant, offlineCloud queue; time on the device is a scarce shared resource
Best used forLearning, developing, debugging, verifying an algorithm is correct in principleGenuine quantum-hardware research and benchmarking what today's devices can actually do
Honest framing. We are in what the field calls the NISQ era — Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum. Today's machines have enough qubits to be interesting and far too much noise to be trustworthy for deep circuits. Fault-tolerant quantum computing — where many physical qubits are bundled into one reliable logical qubit through error correction — is a real and active engineering programme, but it is still ahead of us, not behind us.
Which is why this workshop uses a simulator. When you are learning the ideas, you want the results to reflect your circuit, not the calibration drift of a fridge in another country. Learn the algorithm on a clean simulator first; add a noise model when you want to know how it would survive contact with real hardware.
