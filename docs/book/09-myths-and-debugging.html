Myths & Debugging

Four popular ideas about quantum computing that are worth unlearning — and a practical checklist for when your circuit does something you did not expect.

Four Myths to Leave Behind

Myth 1: "It tries every answer at once"

The claim: a quantum computer puts all possible answers into superposition, evaluates them all simultaneously, and reads out the right one.

The reality: the first half is roughly true and the second half is where it falls apart. You can get all inputs into superposition — but measurement returns exactly one outcome, chosen at random by the probabilities. If that were all there was, you would have an extremely expensive random guesser.

What a real algorithm does is engineer interference (Chapter 7): arrange the phases so the routes to wrong answers cancel and the routes to the right answer reinforce. The parallelism is real; the free readout of all results is not. The whole art is in making the answer you want likely.

Myth 2: "A qubit is just a random bit"

The claim: superposition is only ignorance — a coin spinning in the air that is secretly already heads or tails, and we just have not looked.

The reality: a hidden-but-definite value cannot reproduce what qubits do. The H→Z→H demo settles it: both circuits pass through the same 50/50 state, yet one ends at a certain 0 and the other at a certain 1. A coin that was "secretly heads all along" has no mechanism to produce that difference.

The extra ingredient is phase, and it has no classical counterpart. Amplitudes can cancel; probabilities never can. That is precisely why entanglement violates Bell inequalities — a fact confirmed experimentally and recognised with the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Myth 3: "They'll replace ordinary computers"

The claim: quantum computers are the next generation of computer, and eventually your laptop and phone will be quantum.

The reality: they are specialised accelerators, not general-purpose replacements. A quantum computer offers an advantage only where a problem has structure an interference pattern can exploit — unstructured search, factoring, simulating quantum chemistry and materials, certain optimization and sampling tasks.

For sorting a list, serving a web page, rendering video, or running a spreadsheet, a classical CPU is not merely adequate — it is dramatically better. Expect quantum processors to sit alongside CPUs and GPUs as another cloud-attached accelerator, called on for the narrow slice of work they are good at.

Myth 4: "Entanglement sends signals faster than light"

The claim: measuring one entangled qubit instantly affects its partner light-years away, so you could use it to communicate instantaneously.

The reality: the correlation is real, but it carries no signal. Your own measurement outcome is completely random — you cannot choose to get a 0 rather than a 1. Your partner, looking only at their own results, sees an ordinary random sequence with no way to tell whether you measured, when, or what you got.

The correlation only becomes visible when the two of you compare notes over an ordinary classical channel — a phone call, an email — and that channel is stuck at the speed of light like everything else. Physicists call this the no-communication theorem. Entanglement is beautifully strange; it is not a telegraph.

A useful one-line summary: quantum computing is not about doing everything at once. It is about choreographing amplitudes so that the wrong answers destroy each other and the right one survives.

When Your Circuit Misbehaves

Quantum circuits fail in ways classical code does not, and the usual instinct — add a print statement in the middle — is precisely what you cannot do, because looking collapses the state. Work through this list in order.

  1. Does the import even work? from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator lives in the separate qiskit-aer package, not in qiskit. An ImportError here usually means a missing install or two Qiskit versions fighting in the same environment.
  2. Did you allocate enough qubits and classical bits? QuantumCircuit(2) gives you two qubits and zero classical bits, so there is nowhere for measurement results to land. For a measured circuit you almost always want QuantumCircuit(2, 2).
  3. Did you actually measure? Asking for get_counts() on a circuit with no measure instruction is the single most common beginner error. No measurement means no counts.
  4. What state should exist just before the measurement? Delete the measure lines, run Statevector.from_instruction(qc), and look. This is the closest thing to a debugger you get — and it works only on a simulator, which is one very good reason to develop there.
  5. Are you confusing amplitude with probability? An amplitude of 0.707 is a probability of 0.5, not 0.707. Square the magnitude. Amplitudes can be negative or complex; probabilities are never either.
  6. Did a phase gate change the phase but not the probabilities? Z, S and T leave the measurement statistics in the Z basis completely untouched. If you added one and the histogram did not budge, that is correct behaviour — the effect only appears after a later H lets the paths interfere (Chapter 7).
  7. Are you misreading the bit order? Qiskit prints the highest classical-bit index on the left. '01' means c[1]=0, c[0]=1. If your result looks exactly backwards, it probably is — see Chapter 7.
  8. Is the discrepancy just sampling noise? With 100 shots, a true 50/50 split routinely lands at 44/56. That is not a bug; it is statistics. Raise the shot count before you go hunting for a cause.
  9. Are you comparing an ideal run against a noisy one? Extra outcomes that "should be impossible" and a general blurring of the histogram are the signature of a noise model or real hardware (Chapter 8), not of a logic error in your circuit.
  10. Are you looking at the logical circuit or the transpiled one? transpile() rewrites your gates into the backend's native set and may reorder or remap qubits entirely. If the drawing surprises you, check which version you are drawing.

A Diagnostic Helper

Paste this into your notebook and call it whenever a circuit is not doing what you expect. It answers most of the questions above in one shot.

def diagnose(circuit, name="circuit"):
    """Print the basic facts about a Qiskit circuit, then draw it."""
    print(f"--- {name} ---")
    print("qubits        :", circuit.num_qubits)
    print("classical bits:", circuit.num_clbits)
    print("depth         :", circuit.depth())
    print("operations    :", dict(circuit.count_ops()))

    if circuit.num_clbits == 0:
        print("WARNING: no classical bits - get_counts() will fail.")
    if "measure" not in circuit.count_ops():
        print("WARNING: no measurement - get_counts() will fail.")

    print(circuit.draw(output="text"))
    return circuit


# Example use
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit

qc = QuantumCircuit(2, 2)
qc.h(0)
qc.cx(0, 1)
qc.measure([0, 1], [0, 1])

diagnose(qc, "bell state")
And when the circuit is genuinely fine: drop the measurements and inspect the statevector directly. On a simulator you can see every amplitude and phase — the one debugging superpower real hardware will never give you. 
from qiskit.quantum_info import Statevector

qc_nm = QuantumCircuit(2)     # same circuit, no measurement
qc_nm.h(0)
qc_nm.cx(0, 1)

print(Statevector.from_instruction(qc_nm))
# amplitudes: 0.707 on |00>, 0.707 on |11>, zero elsewhere
